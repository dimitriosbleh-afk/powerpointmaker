  Cultural sensitivity
  The following resources explore Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories and cultures.
  Note that the Australian Curriculum and State variants use the terminology of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and First Nations Australians. This language has been adopted across these resources, and can also be altered to suit your local context.
  Please be aware of the cultural sensitivity of students in your class prior to reading.
  Some material from the texts studied may contain words, descriptions and terms which may be culturally sensitive. Students should be made aware that this language may reflect the views of the period in which the content was created or set, but may not be considered appropriate today.
  Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples should be aware that some reading/viewing material may contain images, voices and names of deceased persons.

  The Bonney Upwelling
  The Bonney Upwelling is a marine phenomenon that occurs between Portland (Victoria) and Robe (South Australia), typically from November to May. During this period, spring winds shift to the southeast and drive an ocean current northwest along the coast, causing surface waters to drift offshore. This movement of water is replaced by cold, nutrient-rich water from Antarctica, which "wells up" from the deep sea floor onto the continental shelf near the coast.
  Sunlight converts these nutrients into food for a variety of marine species, allowing sea animal populations to thrive. This contributes to the biodiversity in the Coorong.
  Image credit: Pixabay

  The vegetation on the Younghusband Peninsula, a part of the Coorong, includes coastal plants like spinifex, coastal daisy, coastal wattle and currant bush.   Ruppia tuberosa
  Ruppia tuberosa is a crucial aquatic plant in the Coorong, providing food for waterbirds and habitat and nourishment for fish and other animals. Historically, Ruppia tuberosa thrived throughout the South Lagoon, with Ruppia beds covering over 1500 hectares. However, a prolonged drought caused a rapid decline in Ruppia populations. While water quality in the Coorong has improved, favourable conditions alone have not led to a natural resurgence due to the diminished seed bank. The Ruppia Translocation Project aims to aid recovery by relocating Ruppia seeds to the South Lagoon to facilitate regrowth.

  Birds
  Over 85 species of waterbirds have been recorded in the Coorong area. The bird population fluctuates throughout the year, but the region is particularly significant for migratory shorebirds like the red-necked stint, sharp-tailed sandpiper, and curlew sandpiper. These migratory birds are protected by international agreements and come from as far as Alaska and Siberia.

  W
  The Coorong ecosystem is unique and complex, home to many vulnerable and endangered species. The large-scale removal of local wetland environments has reduced the number of plants and wildlife that depend on this area. It is home to both local birds and migratory species that travel from as far as Alaska and Siberia. An event known as the Bonney Upwelling occurs due to an influx of water from Antarctica that leaves behind nutrients that many species, particularly marine animals, rely on to survive. The Coorong is a rich environment teeming with various local and migratory species unlike anywhere else in the world.
  Topic sentence (TS) - expresses the paragraphs main idea
  Supporting detail (SD) - provides 3 details that relate to / support the general idea contained in the topic sentence.
  Concluding sentence (CS) - summarises or concludes the paragraph, echoing the idea of the topic sentence but not repeating it.
