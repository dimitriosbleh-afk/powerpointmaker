  Cultural sensitivity
  The following resources explore Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories and cultures.
  Note that the Australian Curriculum and State variants use the terminology of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and First Nations Australians. This language has been adopted across these resources, and can also be altered to suit your local context.
  Please be aware of the cultural sensitivity of students in your class prior to reading.
  Some material from the texts studied may contain words, descriptions and terms which may be culturally sensitive. Students should be made aware that this language may reflect the views of the period in which the content was created or set, but may not be considered appropriate today.
  Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples should be aware that some reading/viewing material may contain images, voices and names of deceased persons.

  Flora of Coorong National Park
  Coorong National Park boasts a diverse array of plant species. Since 1975, 353 plant species have been identified in the park. 132 are non-native to the region, and five are considered rare in South Australia.
  The large-scale removal or degradation of wetlands in the Coorong has led to significant habitat loss for many species. The reduction in wetland area has decreased the availability of breeding and feeding grounds for birds and other wildlife, leading to population declines and, in some cases, local extinctions.

  The Bonney Upwelling
  The Bonney Upwelling is a marine phenomenon that occurs between Portland (Victoria) and Robe (South Australia), typically from November to May. During this period, spring winds shift to the southeast and drive an ocean current northwest along the coast, causing surface waters to drift offshore. This movement of water is replaced by cold, nutrient-rich water from Antarctica, which "wells up" from the deep sea floor onto the continental shelf near the coast.
  Sunlight converts these nutrients into food for a variety of marine species, allowing sea animal populations to thrive. This contributes to the biodiversity in the Coorong.

  Terrestrial species - These plants grow on dry land above the waterline and cannot survive prolonged submersion.

  i
  The vegetation on the Younghusband Peninsula, a part of the Coorong, includes coastal plants like spinifex, coastal daisy, coastal wattle and currant bush. The lagoon and lake edges host salt marsh plants and are surrounded by tea trees. The southern and mainland sections of the park also feature mallee (a type of eucalyptus tree) and grass communities.
  Unfortunately, non-native plants have become problematic in the Coorong. It's estimated that 30% of the plants on the Younghusband Peninsula are non-native, including horehound, bridal creeper, and African boxthorn. These invasive species threaten native plants, especially the amphibious and submerged species that depend on the Coorong’s water quality and levels.

  Ruppia tuberosa
  Ruppia tuberosa is a crucial aquatic plant in the Coorong, providing food for waterbirds and habitat and nourishment for fish and other animals. Historically, Ruppia tuberosa thrived throughout the South Lagoon, with Ruppia beds covering over 1500 hectares. However, a prolonged drought caused a rapid decline in Ruppia populations. While water quality in the Coorong has improved, favourable conditions alone have not led to a natural resurgence due to the diminished seed bank. The Ruppia Translocation Project aims to aid recovery by relocating Ruppia seeds to the South Lagoon to facilitate regrowth.

  Birds
  Over 85 species of waterbirds have been recorded in the Coorong area. The bird population fluctuates throughout the year, but the region is particularly significant for migratory shorebirds like the red-necked stint, sharp-tailed sandpiper, and curlew sandpiper. These migratory birds are protected by international agreements and come from as far as Alaska and Siberia.
  Other birds that reside in the Coorong year-round include stilts, avocets, plovers, lapwings, oystercatchers, ducks, and swans. Additionally, around 2000 Cape Barren geese visit the area during the summer, and rare birds like the fairy tern and little tern nest on the islands in the Coorong.
  Image credit: Pixabay

  Information Text: Coorong National Park
  Sources: Coorong National Park Teacher Resource Pack

  i
  Several species, including pelicans, swans, and ibis, nest in the area. During a good breeding season, 3000 to 4000 pelicans gather on the islands that serve as pelican nurseries. Human impact on wetland areas poses the biggest threat to migratory wader birds. Australia has lost nearly half of its wetlands in the last 200 years. Fishing lines and hooks, discarded nets, 4WDs along coastal areas, and pollution all negatively affect waders and their habitat.
  Pelicans form breeding colonies on secluded islands or shorelines, where they perform unique courtship rituals. Males try to win females' affection by swinging their bills from side to side or throwing small objects, such as sticks and fish, into the air. Pelican breeding numbers are a good indicator of the health of the Coorong. Recently, researchers have used drones to monitor pelican breeding in Coorong National Park.

  Mammals
  The Coorong is home to various mammals, including seals, sea lions, echidnas, wombats, possums, bats, kangaroos, wallabies, and native rats. Some of these species are rare or vulnerable in South Australia. Non-native mammals like cats, foxes, hares, deer, and rabbits are also present.
  Long-nosed Fur Seals
  In recent years, long-nosed fur seals have started appearing in the Coorong. These curious seals have impacted the local fishing industry and the Ngarrindjeri people, the area's traditional owners. The state government is researching ways to reduce conflicts between seals, fishers, and the community.

  Reptiles
  The Coorong provides a good habitat for reptiles, especially lizards and snakes. The heath goanna is considered vulnerable in the state.
  Amphibians
  Frogs play an important role in the Coorong ecosystem. They help control algae growth, eat insects, and provide food for other animals like herons and egrets. Several frog species inhabit the Coorong, with the common froglet being the most prevalent. The banjo frog and spotted marsh frog also reside in the area.

  Fish and Other Invertebrates
  The Coorong is home to 93 different fish species. However, changes to river flow, water quality, and the introduction of non-native fish like carp, perch, and mosquito fish have affected native fish populations. Other small animals without backbones, known as macroinvertebrates, are also found in the Coorong. They are an important food source for fish and shorebirds, and their presence indicates the overall health of the environment.
  In conclusion, Coorong National Park hosts a rich and diverse array of fauna, each contributing to its unique ecosystem. Conservation efforts are essential to maintaining this balance and protecting these species from the various threats they face.

  What it is and where it is located
  An integral part of the Murray-Darling basin, the Coorong is located at the end of the Murray River. It is more than 100 km long and is separated from the ocean by a length of sandy shore named the Younghusband Peninsula. It is a lagoon that contains a mix of freshwater, brackish water, and saltwater across its various stretches of water. This is because it sits between the salty water of the ocean and the freshwater rivers of the basin. The Coorong is a large expanse of different types of water that spans the South-Eastern coast of South Australia.

  Cultural significance
  The Coorong, named ‘Kurangk’ by the Ngarrindjeri people, is of enormous cultural significance. In 2009, the Kungun Ngarrindjeri Yunnan (KNY) agreement was signed between the Ngarrindjeri people and the state government. This agreement recognises traditional ownership of the lands and establishes official processes for Aboriginal rights and responsibilities in regards to the Coorong. The parks are currently managed with the Ngarrindjeri people to honour and preserve this area's rich cultural heritage. By prioritising the conservation and custodianship of the Coorong, both its cultural and ecological importance can be preserved.
