# Auslan Game Bank v1.1
## Companion to AUSLAN_1_UNIT_PROMPT.md | Voice-off games for a signing primary classroom | Grows across units

# What this is

A repository of games that work in an Auslan-only room, held outside the unit prompt so it can grow without making the prompt longer every term. Paste it into the chat alongside AUSLAN_1_UNIT_PROMPT.md when building a unit. The unit's section 10 draws from it, gives each game the appendix number it will keep, and adds any new games that unit invents.

If the bank is not pasted, the unit builder writes its own games from scratch. Nothing breaks. The bank exists so the same proven mechanics come back rather than being reinvented at a lower standard each time.

Two kinds of entry:
- Teacher-supplied. Games this teacher already runs successfully with his own classes. Verified classroom practice. Reproduce the mechanics faithfully and reword only to fit the unit's vocabulary.
- Adapted. Mechanics taken from a published source and rebuilt for a signing room. The adaptation is the work; the mechanic is borrowed and attributed.

# The four things that change in a signing room

These apply to every entry and are not repeated in full each time. Most published games come from spoken-language classrooms and assume two things a signing room does not have: that students can talk while their hands are busy, and that they can call across a space.

1. Hands free. A student cannot hold a card and sign. Cards go flat on a desk, in a card holder, in a lanyard or in a pocket. A mingle game where everyone is clutching a handful of cards does not run.
2. Eye contact before content. Every exchange opens with the class attention protocol, a wave or a table tap, and waits for the eyes. Nobody starts signing at someone's back or across a room. Build the wait into the rules so it is the game, not an interruption to it.
3. Watch, then record. Receiving a sign and writing or drawing at the same time is impossible, and a student who is writing has missed the answer. Sequence it: pencils down, watch, then write. Say it in the rules and enforce it for the first round.
4. Sight lines, and hide the card not the person. Circles and horseshoes, never rows, never a back to a signer. In an information gap game the hidden thing is the card, so lay it flat or stand a low folder in front of it. Never put a screen between two signers; it blocks the language and the game stops.

Also: light on faces, so no signer works with a window behind them, and cap the size of a moving group so every exchange has room for two people to face each other.

# Entry format

Each entry uses these labels, in this order, each on its own line. The shape is adapted from Hadfield's teacher's notes.

Type of activity, What it practises, Target language, Signs needed, Equipment, How to play, Voice off, Safety and rules, Variations, Source.

Games in this bank have no fixed number. The unit's section 10 numbers them in the order that unit first uses them, and that number then belongs to the unit document.

Target language is always plain English. Glosses are listed under Signs needed and never assembled into a signed sentence. Where a game needs a form this bank cannot verify, the entry says so and the teacher models it from the school reference.

# Adding to the bank

Add a game once it has been run and it worked. Write it in the entry format, mark it Teacher-supplied with the date, and put the voice-off adaptation in even when it seems obvious, because the next person building a unit will not have watched it run.

# Teacher-supplied games

## Bob Virus

Type of activity: whole class, search.
What it practises: asking for and giving a name, and fingerspelling under time pressure.
Target language: What is your name? answered by fingerspelling a name.
Signs needed: YOUR NAME WHAT? as the teacher supplies it, plus the fingerspelling alphabet. The extension adds WHO BOB? and WHO NORMAL?
Equipment: a cleared floor space and a timer the whole class can see.
How to play:
1. Students stand in the cleared space with their eyes closed.
2. Tap one or two students on the shoulder. They are the Original Bobs. Everyone else is a normie.
3. On the start signal, students move around the space and ask each other YOUR NAME WHAT?
4. A normie answers by fingerspelling their real name. A Bob answers by fingerspelling B-O-B.
5. Anyone who meets a Bob becomes a Bob.
6. Play for a set time. Two minutes is enough. Run the timer where everyone can see it.
7. At the end, sign WHO BOB? and every Bob raises a hand, Original Bobs included. Sign WHO NORMAL? and the rest raise a hand. The bigger group wins.
Voice off: the whole game, and the mechanic depends on it, because a fingerspelled name only counts if the partner watched it land. Clear the furniture rather than working around it. Students need to face each other squarely and stand close enough to read fingerspelling, so cap the space rather than letting it spread.
Safety and rules: no running, no avoiding a person who approaches you, no voicing.
Variations: the extension adds Doctors. Two taps on the shoulder makes a Doctor, one tap makes a Bob. Doctors introduce themselves by fingerspelling D-R and turn Bobs back into normies. Original Bobs stay Bobs all game and Doctors cannot be infected. At the end Doctors count with the normies.
The mechanic also carries any two-part exchange with one marked answer. Swap the name exchange for How are you? with emotion signs, grumpy standing in for Bob and I am well standing in for the Doctor.
Source: Teacher-supplied.

## More or Less

Type of activity: pairs, guessing.
What it practises: signing numbers, and responding with a comparison.
Target language: a guess, answered with more, less or correct.
Signs needed: MORE, LESS, CORRECT, and the number signs across the agreed range.
Equipment: one mini whiteboard and marker per student.
How to play:
1. Set the number range and leave it displayed, for example 1 to 20.
2. Each student writes a number inside the range on their whiteboard and keeps it hidden from their partner.
3. Player A signs a guess. Player B answers MORE, LESS or CORRECT.
4. Players alternate turns so both are guessing and both are answering.
5. The first to guess their partner's number wins the round. Play again, and the other player guesses first.
Voice off: partners sit facing each other with their boards face down between turns. The board holds the hidden number only. A guess is signed, never written, or the game stops practising number signs.
Variations: the range is the band calibration. A Foundation pair works to 10; older bands go past 100, or into money, or into fractions of an hour, using whatever number set the unit is teaching.
Source: Teacher-supplied.

## Mix and Mingle

Type of activity: whole class, search.
What it practises: opening an exchange with a stranger, asking one set question and holding the answer.
Target language: one question everybody asks, for example What did you do on the weekend? or What is your favourite sport? answered from the vocabulary the unit is practising.
Signs needed: the question form as the teacher supplies it, plus the answer set from the unit's vocabulary list.
Equipment: a timer the whole class can see, and the journal or mini whiteboard for the record at the end.
How to play:
1. Set the question. Everyone asks the same one. Display the English question and start a two or three minute timer where everyone can see it.
2. Students stand, voices off, and move around the space. Approach one person, wait for their eyes, ask the question, watch the whole answer.
3. Both ask and both answer before the pair splits up.
4. When the timer ends, everyone sits. Each student records one name and that person's answer in their journal or on their board.
5. The teacher asks three students who they spoke to and what the answer was. Those three sign it back to the class.
Voice off: the whole activity, and the record at the end is why: a student who did not watch the answer has nothing to write. Approach one person at a time; no queueing behind somebody's back. Cap the space so pairs can square up close enough to read fingerspelling.
Safety and rules: no running, no avoiding a person who approaches you, no voicing.
Variations: Find the same. The goal is to find people whose answer matches yours and stand with them by the end of the timer. At the end the teacher gives each group one question, for example What is your favourite sport? and the group signs the same answer together. It works for any vocabulary set that sorts students into groups: sports, pets, favourite foods, months of birth.
The teacher uses this mechanic under several names for different question sets; it is one routine.
Source: Teacher-supplied.

## Watch and Write

Type of activity: whole class, receptive check.
What it practises: receiving a signed item and recording it, under the whole-class response routine.
Target language: none produced by students; the teacher signs a number, a price, a fingerspelled word or an item from the core vocabulary, and students write what they received.
Signs needed: whatever set is being checked. It began as number and money practice and it carries any core vocabulary and fingerspelling reception.
Equipment: one mini whiteboard and marker per student, or the exercise book with a numbered frame on the screen when the result is being kept.
How to play:
1. Pens down. The teacher signs one item.
2. Students write it. Nobody writes while the teacher is signing.
3. The teacher cues: One, two, three, chin it. Every board comes up under the chin at once.
4. The teacher scans, names the strategy for one right answer, and re-signs the item once if the room split.
5. Ten items is a round. In the exercise book version, the class corrects together at the end and the teacher looks twice only at the outliers.
Voice off: the routine works because the response is written, not spoken; a student who voices the answer gives it to the room. Boards face down between items so the record cannot be copied from a neighbour. Watch the whole item, then write.
Safety and rules: not needed.
Variations: the exercise book version is the unit's Piece 2 receptive test. The board version is the Do Now retrieval and the light check in any lesson. For fingerspelling, sign names and place names from the unit.
Source: Teacher-supplied.

# Adapted games

## How Many In My Room

Type of activity: pairs, information gap.
What it practises: asking how many there are of something, and answering with a number.
Target language: How many chairs are there? There are seven.
Signs needed: the number signs in range, plus the object signs the unit is teaching. Question form modelled by the teacher from the school reference.
Equipment: two versions of the same scene, A and B, with different quantities of each object; one per student. A propped folder per pair.
How to play:
1. Pairs sit facing each other. Each student gets picture A or picture B and lays it flat, screened by a folder standing on the desk.
2. Partners take turns asking how many of each object are in the other's room.
3. Each records the partner's number next to that object on their own sheet.
4. When the list is done, both add their totals and compare. The bigger total wins.
5. Finish with one funny item hidden in both pictures, spiders or socks, and ask who has more of those. It is the ending the class remembers.
Voice off: the folder hides the picture, never the partner. Both faces stay clear and both pairs of hands stay empty, so the sheet is on the desk and the pencil is down while the partner signs. Watch the whole answer, then write. A pair who writes while watching will be a number out within three turns and will blame each other.
Variations: this is the number practice slot for almost any topic. Swap the room for a lunchbox, a pencil case, a zoo or a car park depending on the unit's vocabulary. Widen the number range for older bands.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 4 How many?, page 7. The book's own kitchen picture pair can be photocopied if the unit's vocabulary suits it.

## What Time There

Type of activity: small group, information gap.
What it practises: asking and telling the time.
Target language: It is four o'clock here. What time is it there?
Signs needed: the time and number signs the unit is teaching, plus the place names as the teacher establishes them.
Equipment: one set of four information cards, one set of time cards, and a rules card per group of three or four.
How to play:
1. Groups of four sit in a circle so every player can see every other player. Time cards go face down in the middle.
2. Each player takes one information card, which tells them the time in one place only.
3. A player takes a time card from the middle. It shows the time in one of the places and has blank clock faces for the others.
4. That player asks each of the others in turn what the time is where they are, and fills in the clock faces from the answers.
5. Play passes to the left. For a group of three, the spare information card sits face up in the middle and players take turns answering from it.
Voice off: a circle, not a line, because every player needs the sight line and the turn passes visually. A player takes their turn by getting eye contact, not by being called on. Cards stay on the table. The asker's pencil goes down while the answer is signed and comes back up afterwards.
Safety and rules: one signer at a time. Two people signing at once in a circle is the equivalent of everybody talking, and nobody gets the answer.
Variations: run it with the school day instead of world time zones for younger bands, asking what is happening at each time rather than what the time is elsewhere.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 5 Time zones, page 7. Information cards, time cards and a rules sheet exist in the book's Games material and can be photocopied.

## Where Is It

Type of activity: small group, information gap.
What it practises: asking and answering where something is.
Target language: Where is my ball? It is under the table.
Signs needed: the object signs, and the location signs the unit is teaching. The teacher models the way location is shown from the school reference; this bank does not describe it.
Equipment: one set of room pictures per group of three or four, each showing the same room, plus small pictures of the missing objects. Pencils.
How to play:
1. Groups of three or four. Each player gets a room picture. The big picture is their room; the small pictures around the edge are the things they have lost.
2. Tell them everyone in this house loses everything, which is why the pictures are full of other people's belongings.
3. One player asks the group where one of their lost things is.
4. Whoever can see it in their room answers, and the asker draws it into their own room in that place.
5. The first player to answer takes the next turn, so the turn moves by who was watching.
6. Play until everybody has found all their things.
Voice off: pencils down while anyone is signing, then draw. This game breaks faster than any other in the bank if that rule is loose, because a student head-down drawing has missed both the question and the answer. Group of four maximum, seated so all four faces are visible at once.
Variations: for Foundation, cut it to two lost objects each and prepositions the class has met. For older bands, the finder must give the location without pointing at the picture.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 6 Where are my glasses?, pages 7 to 8. The book's own living room card set can be photocopied.

## Fill In The Street

Type of activity: small group, information gap.
What it practises: giving and receiving a statement about where a place is.
Target language: The post office is next to the bank.
Signs needed: the place signs the unit is teaching, plus the position signs it uses. The teacher models the form from the school reference.
Equipment: one set of four part-complete street plans per group, each holding different information. Pencils.
How to play:
1. Groups of three or four, each with one plan. Nobody may look at another plan.
2. Going around the circle, each player signs one statement about a place on their plan.
3. Everyone else fills that place in on their own plan.
4. A player who cannot use a statement yet writes it in the margin and uses it later.
5. Play continues around the circle until every plan is complete.
Voice off: plans flat on the table and pencils down while the statement is signed, then everybody writes together. The turn passes around the circle in a fixed direction so nobody has to be called on. One signer at a time, and the signer waits for four sets of eyes before starting.
Variations: use the school grounds or the local shopping strip instead of a generic town, which makes the vocabulary worth keeping. Younger bands work with four places, not twelve.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 7 Crossroads, page 8. The book's own plan set and rules sheet can be photocopied.

## Get The Shopping

Type of activity: whole class, exchanging and collecting.
What it practises: asking for something, and saying how much or how many.
Target language: Have you got any apples? Yes. How many do you want? Two.
Signs needed: the food or item signs the unit is teaching, plus number and quantity signs.
Equipment: one shopping list per student, and picture cards for every item on the lists, four dealt to each student. A card holder, tray or desk station per student.
How to play:
1. Give every student a list and four picture cards at random. Almost nothing anyone holds is on their own list.
2. The object is to collect every item on your list by asking other students for it.
3. Approach one person, get their attention properly, ask for one item and say how many.
4. If they have it, they hand the card over. If not, move on. One request per person before moving.
5. Play until the first three students have complete lists, then stop the round.
Voice off: cards never travel in a hand. Each student works from a desk station or a tray, and carries nothing while moving, or they arrive at the next exchange with no hands to sign with. Cap how many students are moving at once, half the class at a time with the other half at their stations, then swap; a full class circulating in a signing room has no room for two people to square up and see each other.
Safety and rules: no running, and no queueing behind someone, because a student waiting behind another student's back cannot see when the exchange has finished.
Variations: works for any collect-a-set vocabulary, a lunch order, a party list, equipment for a sport. Money and totalling turn it into a numeracy crossover for the older bands.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 9 Shopping lists, pages 8 to 9. The book's own list and picture card sets can be photocopied.

## The Wrong Bag

Type of activity: whole class, exchanging and collecting.
What it practises: describing an object well enough that somebody else can pick it out.
Target language: Mine is the big striped one with a red handle. Have you got it?
Signs needed: the colour, size, shape and pattern signs the unit is teaching, plus the object signs.
Equipment: one large picture per student showing four things they own, plus small picture cards of every one of those things, shuffled and dealt four to each student. Card holders or desk stations.
How to play:
1. Everybody went to the same party and everybody went home with the wrong things.
2. Each student gets a large picture, which is what they own, and four small cards, which are what they took by mistake.
3. Students pair up, describe one of their missing things, and ask whether the other person has it.
4. If the description matches a card that student holds, they hand it over. If not, they say so and describe one of their own.
5. Both describe before the pair splits up and finds someone new. Play until students have all four of their own things back.
Voice off: describing is the whole point, so nobody may show their large picture, and nobody may point at a card to ask about it. Cards sit face up at a desk station and students go to each other empty-handed. Pair up by walking over and waiting for eyes, never by waving across the room at somebody already in an exchange.
Variations: the book frames this as ringing everyone up the next morning. Face to face works better here and needs no framing at all. If the class enjoys a story frame, they are meeting at school the next day.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 13 Lost umbrellas, page 10. The book's own picture sets, umbrellas, hats, bags and glasses, can be photocopied.

## Which Family

Type of activity: small group, information gap.
What it practises: describing a person's face and hair.
Target language: My brother has short curly hair and glasses.
Signs needed: the family signs and the appearance signs the unit is teaching.
Equipment: one set of family portrait cards per group of three or four.
How to play:
1. Groups of three or four. Portrait cards are shuffled and laid out face up in the middle where everyone can see them.
2. Each student takes a card naming the family they belong to, and keeps it hidden.
3. In turn, each student describes the people in their family, one at a time.
4. The rest of the group works out which portraits belong to that family and points them out at the end of the description, not during it.
5. The describer says whether the group has it right, then the next player goes.
Voice off: portraits go in the middle of the table, not in anybody's hands. The group watches the whole description before choosing, because a student scanning the portraits mid-description has stopped watching the language. Circle seating so every face is visible.
Care: use the card set. The book suggests running it with students' own family photos, which in a primary classroom means family structure, who lives where and who is missing, becoming the content of a game. If a class does bring in photos, it is a separate planned lesson with the same care rules the unit header sets, and it is never a surprise on the day.
Variations: for older bands the describer answers questions instead of giving a monologue, so the group has to ask rather than wait.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 14 Family portraits, pages 10 to 11. The book's own portrait cards can be photocopied.

## Who Is That One

Type of activity: pairs, information gap.
What it practises: describing what somebody is wearing, and asking which one.
Target language: Who is the girl in the yellow jacket? Which one? The one with the long hair.
Signs needed: the clothing and appearance signs the unit is teaching, plus colours.
Equipment: two versions of the same crowd scene, A and B. On A some people are named; on B a different set is named. One per student, plus a propped folder per pair.
How to play:
1. Pairs sit facing each other, each with one version flat on the desk behind a folder.
2. Both are at the same event and know some of the people there but not all of them.
3. Taking turns, each describes a person they cannot name and asks who it is.
4. The partner finds that person on their own sheet and gives the name, or asks which one and gets a second detail.
5. Each writes the name onto their sheet. Play until both sheets are fully named.
Voice off: the folder hides the sheet, never the partner. Pointing at your own sheet tells your partner nothing, so the description has to carry it, which is the point of the game. Pencils down while the partner signs.
Care: keep it to the picture set. Describing what people are wearing turns very easily into remarks about real classmates, so the rule is that everyone described lives on the sheet.
Variations: which one is the useful bit. Older bands are not allowed to give the name until the asker has produced two details, which forces the clarifying exchange.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 15 Wallflowers, page 11. The book's own paired party scenes can be photocopied.

## Draw My Route

Type of activity: small group, information gap.
What it practises: giving and following directions.
Target language: Go straight ahead, then take the second turning on the left.
Signs needed: the direction and place signs the unit is teaching. The teacher models how a route is given from the school reference.
Equipment: one set of maps per group, each map marking one student's house. Pencils.
How to play:
1. Groups of three or four, one map each. A friend is arriving at the station and visiting every house in turn.
2. Student A gives directions from the station to A's house.
3. Everyone else draws that leg of the route onto their own map.
4. Student B then gives directions from A's house to B's, and so on around the group.
5. At the end, compare maps. Every route should match. Then each student works out the way back to the station.
Voice off: pencils down, watch the whole route, then draw. A route signed while three people are drawing is a route nobody receives. If the group loses it, the giver repeats the whole leg rather than the last step, because there is no way to ask for just the last bit without stopping everyone.
Variations: use a map of the school or the streets around it. For Foundation, one turn and one landmark is a whole route.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 19 Visiting friends, page 12. The book's own map sets can be photocopied.

## Find Your Family

Type of activity: whole class, matching and combining.
What it practises: asking and answering about family members, ages and where people live.
Target language: What is your mother's name? How old is your brother? Where do you live?
Signs needed: the family signs, number signs for ages, and the place signs the unit is teaching.
Equipment: one role card per student, dealt so the cards make up complete families with none left over. Card holders or lanyards.
How to play:
1. Every student gets a card telling them their own name, their family members' names, their age and where they live.
2. Several families share names on purpose, so one matching detail is not enough. Two families may both have a John and a Mary.
3. Students move around the room asking each other about their families.
4. The object is to find every member of your own family, which means checking more than one detail before deciding.
5. A found family stands together and keeps checking new arrivals. Play until every family is complete.
Voice off: cards in a holder or lanyard, never in a hand. As families form they become standing clusters that block sight lines, so families sit down together once complete rather than staying in the middle of the floor. Approach one person at a time and wait for eyes.
Care: these are card families and that is deliberate. It keeps a game about who belongs to whom away from the real families in the room.
Variations: for Foundation, run three families of two with one obvious matching detail each.
Source: adapted from Jill Hadfield, Elementary Communication Games, game 3 The seven families game, pages 6 to 7. The book's own family cards can be photocopied.
